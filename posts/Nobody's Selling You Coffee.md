I ordered a coffee from AB Coffee off my phone the other day, timed to land exactly when I'd walk in. No line, no standing around, cup ready. My first reaction was the one I think I was supposed to have: _this is just better._ My second reaction, a few minutes later, was the one that actually stuck: nothing about making that coffee got faster. The same machine, the same barista, the same forty-five seconds of extraction. So where did the wait go?

It didn't go anywhere. It moved. It used to sit in a line, visible, mine, annoying. Now it sits somewhere between placing the order and walking in — except I don't experience it as waiting anymore, because I'm not standing still for it. AB Coffee didn't make the coffee faster. It made the same wait invisible, and then it charged me a subscription for the privilege of not seeing it.

That's a more interesting sentence than "they optimized their operations," and once I noticed it I couldn't stop seeing it everywhere.

---

**The wait didn't shrink. It got relocated.**

Here's the thing nobody selling you a "seamless experience" wants said out loud: capacity doesn't change because an app exists. The barista still makes one coffee at a time. The queue is a law of physics, not a UX problem — somebody, at some point, is going to be the marginal cup being made while everyone else's order sits in a buffer. What pre-ordering actually does is take that unavoidable wait and move it to a place where you don't feel it as waiting: a notification on your phone instead of a body standing in a line. Same total time. Completely different experience of that time.

And the subscription sitting next to it isn't really a discount. It's a second version of the same trick, aimed at money instead of time. Once you've paid monthly, the marginal cup feels free — you stop doing the mental math of "is this coffee worth ₹180 today," because you already paid for it in the abstract, three weeks ago. The wait got hidden. The cost got hidden. Neither actually disappeared. Both just stopped being something you consciously feel at the moment you'd normally feel them.

Once I saw that pattern, I went looking for a business doing it on purpose, at a much higher stakes level than coffee. I found one.

---

**Superhealth is the same trick played for real money.**

Superhealth runs maternity, dental, and skin care in Bangalore on a model they call things like "Superbirth" — one bundled fee that covers the entire pregnancy, delivery, and the baby's first year: every scan, every appointment, every vaccination, a full care team on call. On the surface that's just a subscription, same as AB Coffee. But three of their actual design choices tell you they're doing something more deliberate than bundling a price.

First: they cap how many couples they take on each month. Not because they're capacity-constrained in some accidental way — they say it outright, "limited by design," so that every couple gets real time and attention. That's a company turning away revenue on purpose. You don't do that to save money. You do it to protect a feeling.

Second: their doctors are salaried, with no sales targets and no commissions. That's an incentive fix, not a cost decision — it's there so a patient never has to wonder if a recommendation is about her or about someone's quota. Compare that to what happens when incentives aren't fixed: food delivery riders paid purely per-drop, racing recklessly because the pay structure rewards speed over everything else. Same underlying mistake, opposite fix.

Third: bundled pricing again, but at a much higher-stakes moment than a cup of coffee — a pregnancy. The whole point is that a new mother should never be doing cost-benefit math on whether to call her doctor about something that's probably nothing. The bundle removes exactly that moment of hesitation.

None of this is about running a more efficient hospital. A more efficient hospital takes on _more_ patients per doctor, not fewer. What Superhealth is actually selling is the feeling of being unhurried and personally looked after in the most anxious period of someone's life — and every operational decision they've made is in service of manufacturing that feeling, even at the direct cost of growth.

That's the real thesis, once you strip the framework language off it: **the product isn't the coffee, and it isn't the medical care. The product is the engineered absence of friction — no visible wait, no moment of doubt about cost, no anxiety about whether you're a priority. And that feeling is being built on purpose, using tools that look like cost-cutting but are actually being spent on experience design.**

---

**Quick commerce is the same bet, except nobody's earned it yet.**

There's a mirror version of this happening right now with 10-minute grocery delivery, and it's a useful contrast because it shows what it looks like when a company wants the same feeling but hasn't paid for it honestly.

Picture the classic operations triangle: cost, variety, and response time, where you can really only hold two at once. Low cost and wide variety cost you speed. Fast and wide-variety costs you money. Fast and cheap costs you variety. Quick commerce is sitting at the expensive corner right now — fast delivery, huge variety, and the wait genuinely is short — and it's _not_ passing that cost onto you. Someone else is eating it. Every 10-minute order is subsidized; margins across the big players are still negative or barely positive per order.

That's not an accident, it's a bet. The bet is that if they hold that expensive corner long enough, burning investor cash the whole time, enough people will stop opening five different apps to compare prices — enough people will just default to one — that the company earns enough density and loyalty to eventually slide toward the cheap-and-fast corner on real economics instead of subsidy. AB Coffee and Superhealth _designed_ their way into a sustainable version of "no friction." Quick commerce is trying to _buy_ the same feeling with someone else's money, betting that the loyalty shows up before the money runs out.

Nobody knows yet which one wins. That's honestly the more interesting question than whichever company gets written about this month.

---

**The actual question worth asking**

I don't think the next decade of consumer businesses gets decided by who has the better product, or even who has the smarter logistics. Everyone will eventually have decent logistics — that part's a solved problem, or close enough. What's not solved is this: who correctly bets on whether the feeling they're selling has to be _earned_, through the kind of deliberate, sometimes revenue-costing design choices Superhealth is making — or can be _bought_, temporarily, with enough capital to outlast the wait for the economics to catch up.

I don't know the answer yet. I know which one I'd bet on if I were building something instead of just watching from the outside — but that's probably a different post.
